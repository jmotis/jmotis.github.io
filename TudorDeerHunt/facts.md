## Facts

### Childhood, 1491–1509

- Henry was born on June 28, 1491 in Greenwich. The castle in the background of this game is Greenwich Castle, but he was born in the nearby royal Palace of Placentia.
- Henry was the second son of King Henry VII of England and Queen Elizabeth of York. According to the laws of primogeniture, Elizabeth was arguably the rightful heir to the throne on the death of her father and brothers. Henry VII won the throne at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485 then married her to secure his dynasty.
- Henry had an older brother, Arthur, an older sister, Margaret, and a younger sister, Mary, who survived to be at least teenagers.
- As a small child, Henry was invested with numerous honors, including being made the Duke of York at age three.
- When Henry was ten years old, his older brother Arthur married a princess named Catherine of Aragon, the daughter of King Ferdinand II of Aragon and Queen Isabella I of Castile (who famously sponsored Christopher Columbus' voyages to the New World).
- In 1502, his older brother Arthur died at the age of 15 and Henry became heir to the throne.

### The young king, 1509–1511

- Henry's father died on April 22, 1509, leaving the seventeen-year-old to take the throne as Henry VIII. This was the first uncontested succession in over half a century.
- Henry inherited a stable kingdom and a full treasury, thanks to his father's careful (and sometimes ruthless) handling of money. Henry spent most of that money within a few years.
- The young Henry was over six feet tall, athletic, and considered very handsome, with reddish-gold hair. He was also stubborn and had a fierce temper.
- Henry married Catherine of Aragon, his brother's widow, on June 11, 1509, only weeks after becoming king. The marriage kept England allied with Spain, a rising power in Europe.
- Catherine's mother, Isabella, ruled Castile in her own right, and Catherine's sister Joanna inherited that throne after her. Catherine grew up seeing nothing strange about a woman ruling a kingdom.
- Henry and Catherine were crowned together at Westminster Abbey on June 24, 1509.
- Unlike his cautious father, Henry preferred a public, ceremonial court full of feasts, tournaments, and music to being surrounded by a few close friends. He believed that he ruled by divine right, and he left most of the daily work of government to his advisers.
- In 1510, Henry executed Richard Empson and Edmund Dudley, his father's hated tax collectors. It was an easy way for a new king to become popular.
- Henry loved music. He played the lute, the organ, and the virginals (an early keyboard instrument), and he composed songs, including "Pastime with Good Company."
- Henry was an enthusiastic jouster, tennis player, and hunter. He kept deer parks at his palaces, and hunting parties like the one in this game were a central part of life at court.
- Henry's court attracted scholars as well as athletes. The famous Dutch scholar Erasmus visited England several times and praised the young king's learning.
- On January 1, 1511, Catherine gave birth to a son, Henry, Duke of Cornwall. The king celebrated with a great tournament at Westminster, but the baby died after only 52 days.

### War with France and Scotland, 1511–1515

- In 1511, Henry joined the Holy League, an alliance against France. He dreamed of winning back English lands in France and matching the military glory of Henry V.
- To fight France, Henry built up the navy. He founded new dockyards at Woolwich and Deptford and built great warships such as the Mary Rose.
- In 1512, Henry sent an army to Spain to help his father-in-law, Ferdinand of Aragon, attack France. Ferdinand used the English as a distraction while he conquered Navarre for himself, then made his own peace with France. The sick and badly supplied English troops mutinied and sailed home.
- Thomas Wolsey, said to be the son of an Ipswich butcher, studied at Oxford and entered royal service in 1507. He rose quickly because he was very good at organizing Henry's wars.
- To encourage Henry to attack France, the Pope secretly granted him the title "King of France." Parliament then voted extra money for the war.
- In June 1513, Henry personally led an army across the Channel, becoming the first English king to campaign in France since Henry V. His ally, the Holy Roman Emperor Maximilian, showed up with only about 2,000 soldiers.
- On August 16, 1513, the English routed the French cavalry at the Battle of the Spurs. The battle got its name from how quickly the French spurred their horses to flee.
- Henry captured the French towns of Thérouanne and Tournai in 1513. Tournai was sold back to France only a few years later.
- While Henry was in France, Catherine of Aragon governed England as regent. When the Scots invaded, she stripped the Tower of London of armor and raised an army in three divisions, planning to lead the rearguard herself.
- On September 9, 1513, the English crushed the Scots at the Battle of Flodden. King James IV of Scotland, the husband of Henry's sister Margaret, was killed along with thousands of his men.
- Catherine thought about sending James IV's body to Henry in France as a trophy. She sent the dead king's bloodied coat instead.
- James IV left a one-year-old son, James V, as King of Scotland. The years that followed were full of power struggles, and in 1517 Henry's sister Margaret even sided with the Scottish regent, the Duke of Albany, against her own brother. James V did not take control of his kingdom until 1528.
- Until Henry had children of his own, his heir by the laws of primogeniture was his older sister Margaret, whose husband his army had just killed. The English distrusted the idea of a Scottish succession, and Henry preferred the family of his younger sister Mary.
- The wars of 1512 to 1514 cost more than £900,000, at a time when the crown's income was only about £150,000 a year.
- In 1514, Henry made peace with France. As part of the deal, his eighteen-year-old sister Mary married the elderly King Louis XII of France, but only after Henry promised that she could choose her next husband herself.
- Louis XII died less than three months after the wedding. Mary then secretly married the man she loved, Charles Brandon, Duke of Suffolk, who was one of Henry's closest friends.
- Marrying a royal princess without the king's permission was treason. Henry was furious, but he forgave Mary and Suffolk after they agreed to pay him a very large fine. Their granddaughter, Lady Jane Grey, would briefly claim the English throne in 1553.
- Louis XII was succeeded by Francis I, a king even younger than Henry and just as eager for glory. The two were rivals for the rest of their lives.
- In 1514, Henry took his first known mistress, Jane Popincourt, a Frenchwoman who had been his sisters' tutor. Henry was not especially promiscuous for a king: he usually took a mistress while his wife was pregnant and then returned to his wife.

### Wolsey's England, 1514–1525

- Wolsey became Archbishop of York in 1514, a cardinal in 1515, and Lord Chancellor of England in December 1515. He now held more power in both church and state than anyone except the king.
- Anyone who wanted something done in Henry's government had to go through Wolsey. This made him enormously unpopular, but he was confident that the king would always protect him.
- At the height of his power, Wolsey's income was about £35,000 a year. That was roughly five times the income of the richest nobles and about a third of the king's own.
- Around 1515, Wolsey began building Hampton Court Palace. It later passed to Henry, who added a great hall, enormous kitchens, tennis courts, and an astronomical clock.
- Princess Mary was born on February 18, 1516. Catherine of Aragon was pregnant at least six times, but Mary was the only one of her children to survive infancy.
- In 1516, Henry's friend and adviser Thomas More published Utopia, a book describing an imaginary island with an ideal society. The word "utopia" comes from this book.
- In 1517, a German monk named Martin Luther published his Ninety-Five Theses criticizing the Catholic Church. This began the Protestant Reformation, which would eventually transform England too.
- In 1518, Henry granted a charter founding the Royal College of Physicians, the first organization to license doctors in England. Its first president was his own physician, Thomas Linacre.
- In 1518, Wolsey was made a papal legate, which meant he held the Pope's authority in England. He now served two masters, the king and the Pope, whose interests did not always match.
- Also in 1518, Wolsey arranged the Treaty of London, an ambitious agreement meant to keep peace among all the major powers of Europe. Hosting peace talks let England look important far more cheaply than fighting wars did.
- In 1519, Henry's mistress Elizabeth Blount gave birth to his son, Henry FitzRoy ("FitzRoy" means "son of the king"). To Henry, this was proof that he could father healthy sons and that the problem lay with Catherine.
- Also in 1519, Catherine's nephew Charles became Holy Roman Emperor as Charles V. He already ruled Spain, the Netherlands, and much of Italy, which made him the most powerful ruler in Europe.
- England was much smaller than France or Charles V's empire, but it could tip the balance between the two giants. Both sides wanted Henry as an ally.
- In June 1520, Henry met Francis I of France near Calais at a summit so lavish that it is known as the Field of the Cloth of Gold. For nearly three weeks the two courts feasted, jousted, and tried to outdo each other.
- The two kings had agreed not to compete against each other directly at the Field of the Cloth of Gold. They wrestled anyway, and Francis threw Henry to the ground.
- Charles V was so worried about the Field of the Cloth of Gold that he visited Henry both right before it and right after it. He relied on his aunt, Catherine of Aragon, to keep England on his side.
- In May 1521, Henry had the Duke of Buckingham executed for treason. Buckingham was the richest noble in England and had royal blood, which made him a possible rival for the throne.
- In 1521, Henry wrote a book called the Defence of the Seven Sacraments attacking Martin Luther's ideas. He may have had help from Thomas More, but he was proud of his knowledge of theology and claimed the book as his own.
- On October 11, 1521, a grateful Pope Leo X gave Henry the title "Defender of the Faith." British monarchs still use the title today, and its abbreviation (F.D.) appears on British coins.
- In 1521, Henry secretly agreed to join Charles V in another war against France. Wolsey may have hoped to become Pope himself, and part of the deal was a promise that Charles would support him.
- As part of the alliance, six-year-old Princess Mary was betrothed to her cousin Charles V, who was sixteen years older than she was.
- Around 1522, a young woman named Anne Boleyn came home to England after years at the French court. She was known for her wit, her dark eyes, and her fashionable French manners.
- English armies invaded France in 1522 and 1523 alongside Charles V's forces. They gained almost nothing, and the cost was enormous.
- In February 1525, Charles V's army crushed the French at the Battle of Pavia and captured King Francis I himself. Henry wanted to invade France and divide it up, but Charles had no interest in sharing his victory.
- To pay for a new invasion of France in 1525, Henry and Wolsey demanded a special tax called the Amicable Grant without asking Parliament. People resisted so strongly that the king had to cancel it.
- Henry could not afford the dowry he had promised, so Charles V dropped his engagement to Princess Mary and married a Portuguese princess who came with plenty of money. Humiliated, Henry made peace with France later in 1525.

### The succession problem, 1525–1527

- In 1525, Henry made six-year-old Henry FitzRoy the Duke of Richmond and Somerset. No illegitimate son of an English king had ever been given such high honors. Henry even considered marrying FitzRoy to his half-sister Mary to settle the succession.
- Also in 1525, Henry gave nine-year-old Mary the privileges of the Prince of Wales, though not the title, and sent her to Wales as if she were his heir.
- Henry feared leaving his throne to a daughter. England's only precedent was the Empress Matilda, who was driven out within weeks of being recognized in 1141 and whose claim led to fourteen years of civil war.
- Catherine of Aragon saw no problem with a female heir. Her own mother had ruled Castile, and she raised Mary to expect to rule England.
- In 1526, Wolsey issued the Eltham Ordinances, a set of rules to reform the royal household. They cut the number of hangers-on at court and tightened control of spending.
- Around 1526, Henry fell in love with Anne Boleyn. Her older sister Mary had already been his mistress, but Anne refused to become his lover unless he married her.
- Henry came to believe that his marriage to Catherine was cursed. The Book of Leviticus says that a man who marries his brother's wife will be childless, and Henry decided that his lack of sons was God's punishment.
- Henry wanted an annulment, not a divorce. An annulment would declare that his marriage to Catherine had never been valid in the first place.
- Henry expected the annulment to be easy. Popes had recently annulled his sister Mary's first betrothal, two of the Duke of Suffolk's earlier marriages, and, in 1527, the second marriage of his sister Margaret.

### The King's Great Matter, 1527–1533

- Henry's effort to end his marriage became known as the King's Great Matter. It dominated English politics for six years.
- In May 1527, Wolsey secretly opened a church court and charged Henry with living in sin with his brother's widow, hoping to settle the matter quickly inside England. He gave up when it became clear that Catherine would fight.
- In May 1527, Charles V's unpaid soldiers sacked the city of Rome and made Pope Clement VII a virtual prisoner. The Pope was not about to insult Charles by declaring that his aunt Catherine had never been truly married.
- Catherine refused any compromise. She remembered her seven miserable years as Arthur's cast-off widow, and she was very popular with the English people.
- In 1528, Henry joined France in declaring war on Charles V. England could not afford to fight, and it was a decision that both Henry and Wolsey came to regret.
- In 1528, an epidemic of the mysterious "sweating sickness" swept England. It could kill within hours. This was the only outbreak that ever spread to the rest of Europe, and it disrupted both the court and the annulment negotiations.
- In 1528, the Pope sent Cardinal Campeggio to England to hear the annulment case alongside Wolsey. Campeggio had secret orders to delay for as long as possible.
- The court finally opened at Blackfriars in London in 1529. Catherine knelt at Henry's feet, swore that she had been a true wife and that her marriage to Arthur had never been consummated, and appealed her case to Rome. Then she walked out, ignoring his shouts for her to come back.
- Cardinal Campeggio then adjourned the court and sent the case to Rome. Henry now knew that he would never get his annulment from the Pope.
- Wolsey's failure to get the annulment destroyed him. In October 1529 he was stripped of his office as Lord Chancellor and replaced by Thomas More.
- Henry took Wolsey's London mansion, York Place, and rebuilt it as Whitehall Palace. It was the main London home of English monarchs until it burned down in 1698.
- Three factions now competed for the king's ear: Catherine's supporters (including Thomas More and Bishop John Fisher), the reformers around Anne Boleyn, and conservative nobles like the Duke of Norfolk, who disliked the divorce but followed the king's lead.
- In November 1529, Henry called the Parliament that became known as the Reformation Parliament. It sat for seven years and passed the laws that separated England from the Catholic Church.
- By using Parliament to change the church, Henry made Parliament more powerful than it had ever been. That caused trouble for later kings and helped lead to a civil war in the next century.
- After his fall, Wolsey retreated to York and began plotting with foreign powers to get his position back. He was arrested for treason but died at Leicester Abbey on November 29, 1530, on his way to trial.
- Henry had a team of scholars search old histories for proof that English kings had always been supreme in their own realm, free from the Pope. He was delighted with the collection of evidence they produced.
- In 1531, Henry accused the entire English clergy of a crime for having accepted Wolsey's authority as the Pope's representative. To be pardoned, they paid a fine of £118,000 and recognized Henry as supreme head of the church "as far as the law of Christ allows."
- Around 1531, Henry began building St. James's Palace in London on the site of a former hospital for lepers. It still stands today.
- Not everyone accepted the king's plans. By 1531 a nun named Elizabeth Barton, known as the Holy Maid of Kent, was publicly prophesying disaster for Henry if he abandoned Catherine.
- In 1532, Parliament threatened to cut off the payments that the Pope received from the English church. That May, the clergy formally gave up the right to make church laws without the king's approval, an event known as the Submission of the Clergy.
- Thomas More resigned as Lord Chancellor the day after the clergy submitted. He could not in good conscience support the attack on the Pope's authority. Henry promised never to trouble any man's conscience over religion, a promise he did not keep.
- In the autumn of 1532, Henry made Anne Boleyn the Marquess of Pembroke in her own right, a remarkable honor for a woman. The couple then traveled to Calais to win the support of Francis I for their marriage.
- By the end of 1532, Anne was pregnant, and Henry was sure the child would be a boy. He married her secretly around January 1533 so that the baby would be born legitimate. He did not consider this bigamy, because in his own mind his first marriage had never been valid.
- In March 1533, Henry made Thomas Cranmer, a supporter of the annulment, the new Archbishop of Canterbury. Ironically, the Pope himself approved the appointment.
- In 1533, the Act in Restraint of Appeals declared that "this realm of England is an empire," meaning that it answered to no outside power. Legal cases, including the king's marriage, could no longer be appealed to Rome.
- In May 1533, Archbishop Cranmer declared Henry's marriage to Catherine invalid and his marriage to Anne lawful. Henry had the annulment that the Pope had refused him for six years.

### Supreme Head of the Church, 1533–1535

- Anne Boleyn was crowned queen on June 1, 1533. Many Londoners watched her procession in cold silence, and the Pope threatened to excommunicate Henry.
- On September 7, 1533, Anne gave birth at Greenwich to a girl, the future Queen Elizabeth I. Henry had been so sure of a son that the birth announcements had already been written for a "prince" and had to be corrected by hand.
- Henry declared his daughter Mary illegitimate and broke up her household. He sent the seventeen-year-old to serve as a lady-in-waiting to her baby half-sister Elizabeth.
- The Act of Succession of 1534 made Anne's children the heirs to the throne. It met stiff resistance in both houses of Parliament, and all adult men were required to swear an oath accepting it.
- In April 1534, Elizabeth Barton, the Holy Maid of Kent, and several of her supporters were executed for treason. They were the first victims of Henry's new laws.
- In 1534, Parliament ended all payments to Rome and redirected the church's taxes to the crown. That November, the Act of Supremacy declared Henry the "Supreme Head of the Church of England," completing England's break with Rome.
- The Treasons Act of 1534 made it a crime punishable by death to call the king a heretic, a tyrant, or a usurper, even in conversation. Words alone could now get a person killed.
- Thomas More and Bishop John Fisher both refused to swear the oath and were imprisoned in the Tower of London in 1534.
- In 1535, a group of Carthusian monks who refused to accept Henry as head of the church were put to death. Bishop Fisher was beheaded on June 22, 1535, just weeks after the Pope had made him a cardinal.
- Thomas More defended himself skillfully at his trial by refusing to say anything at all about the king's new title. He was convicted only after a witness claimed that More had denied the royal supremacy in a private conversation.
- More was beheaded on July 6, 1535. He reportedly said on the scaffold that he died "the King's good servant, but God's first." The executions of More and Fisher shocked Europe.
- The man who managed Henry's religious revolution was Thomas Cromwell, a blacksmith's son who had once worked for Wolsey. In 1535 Henry made him his deputy in all church matters.
- In 1535, Cromwell ordered a survey of the wealth of every church and monastery in England, called the Valor Ecclesiasticus. It has been called the most important dull document since the Domesday Book.

### Three queens in one year, 1536

- Catherine of Aragon died on January 7, 1536, still insisting that she was Henry's true wife and queen. When the news arrived, Henry and Anne reportedly both dressed in yellow, and historians still argue about whether they were celebrating or mourning.
- On January 24, 1536, Henry was thrown from his horse while jousting at Greenwich and lay unconscious for two hours. He never jousted again, and he was troubled for the rest of his life by a leg wound that would not heal.
- A few days after the king's accident, Anne Boleyn miscarried a baby boy. With Catherine dead, Anne's enemies realized that if Anne were gone too, Henry could marry again and nobody could question the new marriage.
- In early 1536, Henry began courting Jane Seymour, one of Anne's ladies-in-waiting. Her family and Anne's enemies coached her to appear quiet and obedient, the opposite of the fiery Anne.
- In 1536, Parliament closed every monastery with an income of less than £200 a year. The monks were given pensions, the crown took the land and valuables, and a new government department, the Court of Augmentations, was created to manage it all.
- Also in 1536, new laws united Wales fully with England, giving the Welsh English law and seats in Parliament.
- In May 1536, Anne Boleyn was arrested and charged with adultery with five men, including her own brother George. Thomas Cromwell arranged the arrests, and a court musician named Mark Smeaton confessed under torture. Most historians today believe the charges were invented.
- Anne Boleyn was beheaded at the Tower of London on May 19, 1536, by an expert swordsman brought over from France.
- Henry became engaged to Jane Seymour the day after Anne's execution and married her on May 30, 1536.
- A new Act of Succession in 1536 declared both Mary and Elizabeth illegitimate. A few weeks later Henry FitzRoy, the king's illegitimate son, died at about seventeen, probably of tuberculosis. Henry now had no heir of any kind.
- Jane Seymour was sympathetic to Catherine of Aragon and worked to reconcile Henry with his daughter Mary. When Jane tried to speak up about politics, however, Henry sharply told her not to meddle, and she did not try again.
- In 1536, Henry issued the Ten Articles, the first statement of what his new church believed. They discussed only three of the seven traditional sacraments (baptism, confession, and the Mass), which alarmed traditional Catholics.
- Monasteries had been a major source of charity. In 1536 Parliament passed a Poor Law providing public support for the "deserving poor," such as the sick, the elderly, widows, and orphans, but not for unemployed men who were able to work.
- In October 1536, William Tyndale, who had translated the New Testament into English, was executed for heresy near Brussels. Henry had tried to ban Tyndale's Bible, yet the English Bible he approved a few years later was largely based on it.

### Rebellion and an heir, 1536–1537

- In 1536, three sets of royal officials were at work in the north of England: closing small monasteries, collecting taxes, and inspecting the clergy. Rumors spread that the king would seize church silver, tax cattle and christenings, and confiscate weapons.
- On October 2, 1536, the people of Louth in Lincolnshire rose in revolt. Within days, about 10,000 men were marching on the city of Lincoln.
- The revolt spread to Yorkshire, where a lawyer named Robert Aske captured the city of York and named the movement the "Pilgrimage of Grace."
- The pilgrims insisted that they were loyal to the king and blamed "evil councillors" like Cromwell. Their demands, the 24 Pontefract Articles, included a return to the Catholic faith, the restoration of the monasteries, and the restoration of Princess Mary as heir.
- By late October the pilgrim army numbered over 30,000 well-armed men. No royal army could have defeated it, so the Duke of Norfolk had to negotiate instead of fight.
- During the crisis, the Earl of Northumberland, who had once been engaged to Anne Boleyn, made the king his heir to prove his loyalty.
- Henry promised the rebels a pardon, and they went home. When a small new rising broke out in early 1537, he used it as an excuse to take his revenge. About 178 people were executed, including Robert Aske, who was hanged in chains at York.
- Reginald Pole, a cousin of the king living in Italy, was made a cardinal and tried to organize foreign help for the Pilgrimage of Grace. Henry tried to have him assassinated but failed.
- On October 12, 1537, Jane Seymour gave birth at Hampton Court to the son Henry had wanted for nearly thirty years, the future King Edward VI.
- Jane Seymour died twelve days later from complications of childbirth. Henry wore black for months, and he later asked to be buried beside her.
- As a widower whose marriages were no longer in dispute, Henry could now have made peace with the Pope. He chose instead to keep control of the English church.

### The end of the monasteries, 1538–1540

- Hans Holbein the Younger, a German artist, was Henry's court painter from about 1536 until his death in 1543. His portrait of Henry standing with his feet planted wide apart is still the image most people have of the king.
- In 1538, Henry ordered the destruction of the shrine of Saint Thomas Becket at Canterbury, the most popular pilgrimage site in England. The saint's bones were scattered and the shrine's treasures were hauled off to the royal treasury.
- In 1538, Pope Paul III excommunicated Henry and declared that his subjects no longer owed him obedience. No foreign ruler acted on it, but Henry lived in fear that France and the Emperor would invade together.
- Unable to reach Reginald Pole, Henry arrested Pole's elderly mother, Margaret, Countess of Salisbury, in 1538. She was one of the last members of the old Plantagenet royal family. After more than two years in the Tower, she was beheaded in 1541.
- Fearing a Catholic invasion, Henry began building a chain of artillery forts along the southern coast in 1539. Several of these low, round castles, including Deal and Walmer, still stand.
- In 1539, the first official English translation of the Bible, the Great Bible, was printed. Henry ordered every parish church to have a copy so that people could hear Scripture in their own language.
- Henry was still Catholic in most of his beliefs. The Act of Six Articles of 1539 upheld traditional teachings such as confession to a priest and the rule that priests could not marry. Protestants nicknamed it the "whip with six strings."
- Between 1538 and 1540, the larger monasteries were closed too. The last, Waltham Abbey, surrendered in March 1540, ending nearly a thousand years of monastic life in England. Many were stripped for building materials, and their ruins still dot the English countryside.
- The dissolution of the monasteries was one of the largest transfers of property in English history. It brought the crown lands worth about £126,000 a year.
- Henry sold most of the monastery lands to nobles and gentry to pay for his wars. The crown lost a permanent income, but the buyers now had a strong financial reason to support the break with Rome.

### Anne of Cleves and Catherine Howard, 1540–1542

- Cromwell arranged Henry's fourth marriage, to the German princess Anne of Cleves, to gain Protestant allies against France and the Emperor. Henry agreed after seeing a flattering portrait of her by Holbein.
- Before their official meeting, Henry disguised himself and surprised Anne of Cleves at her lodgings, a romantic gesture from the tradition of courtly love. The meeting went badly, and he complained that she looked nothing like her portrait.
- Henry married Anne of Cleves in January 1540 but had the marriage annulled six months later. Anne accepted gracefully, received a generous settlement, and was known afterward as "the King's Sister." She outlived Henry and all his other wives.
- Arthur Plantagenet, Viscount Lisle, an illegitimate son of King Edward IV, governed Calais for Henry. In 1540 he was accused of plotting to betray the town and sent to the Tower. Two years later, he died of a heart attack the night before he was to be released.
- Cromwell's enemies took advantage of the failed Cleves marriage. He was arrested in June 1540 and beheaded on July 28. Henry later complained that he had been tricked into destroying his most able servant.
- On the same day that Cromwell was executed, Henry married his fifth wife, Catherine Howard, a niece of the Duke of Norfolk. She was probably still a teenager, and he was 49.
- Because the Howards were a strongly Catholic family, many expected Henry's fifth marriage to bring back the old religion. In fact Henry had never been a friend to Protestants and had been burning them as heretics all along.
- In 1541, the Irish Parliament changed Henry's title from "Lord of Ireland" to "King of Ireland." He tried to bring the Gaelic Irish lords under English law by having them surrender their lands to the crown and receive them back as royal grants.
- In the summer of 1541, Henry made a grand tour of the north of England to show his power in the region that had rebelled during the Pilgrimage of Grace.
- That autumn, Archbishop Cranmer gave Henry evidence that Catherine Howard had had lovers before her marriage and had been meeting Thomas Culpeper, one of the king's favorite attendants, during the northern tour.
- Catherine Howard was beheaded on February 13, 1542. Parliament also made it treason for a future queen to hide her past from the king. Henry is said to have wept over his bad luck with wives.

### The last years, 1542–1547

- By the 1540s, Henry's health had collapsed. His suits of armor show that his waist had grown from about 32 inches to more than 50. He could barely walk and was moved around Whitehall Palace in special wheeled chairs.
- In 1542, Henry went to war with Scotland, which was ruled by his nephew James V. On November 24, 1542, the English won a crushing victory at the Battle of Solway Moss.
- James V died three weeks after Solway Moss, leaving the crown to his six-day-old daughter Mary, Queen of Scots. He supposedly said of his family's hold on the throne, "It came with a lass; it will pass with a lass."
- Henry tried to marry the baby Mary, Queen of Scots, to his son Edward, which would have united the two kingdoms. When the Scots refused in 1543, he sent armies to burn and raid Scotland, a campaign later nicknamed the "Rough Wooing."
- On July 12, 1543, Henry married his sixth and last wife, Catherine Parr, a 31-year-old widow. She had hoped to marry Thomas Seymour, the brother of Jane Seymour, but she could not refuse the king.
- In 1543, Henry had second thoughts about letting everyone read the Bible. A new law allowed only nobles, gentry, and wealthy merchants to read it, which was a disaster for religious reformers.
- The Third Act of Succession of 1544 put Mary and Elizabeth back in line for the throne after Edward. Catherine Parr helped bring Henry's three children together at court.
- In 1544, Henry invaded France one last time and captured the town of Boulogne. Catherine Parr governed England as regent while he was away.
- To pay for his wars, Henry began in 1544 to mix cheap metal into England's silver coins. Prices rose sharply, and people lost trust in their money. As the thin layer of silver wore off the king's portrait, he earned the nickname "Old Coppernose."
- In July 1545, a French fleet attacked the south coast and landed soldiers on the Isle of Wight. As Henry watched from the shore, his great warship the Mary Rose capsized and sank. The wreck was raised from the seabed in 1982.
- In 1545, Catherine Parr published Prayers or Meditations. She was one of the first women in England to publish a book under her own name.
- In 1546, conservatives at court tried to have Catherine Parr arrested for heresy. Warned in time, she threw herself on Henry's mercy and he forgave her.
- In July 1546, a Protestant gentlewoman named Anne Askew was burned for heresy. She had been tortured on the rack in the Tower of London, which was almost unheard of for a woman.
- In December 1546, Henry founded Trinity College at the University of Cambridge. It is still one of the most famous colleges in the world.
- In Henry's last years, the Howard family tried to win back influence by supplying the king with a new mistress. Instead, in December 1546, the Duke of Norfolk and his son the Earl of Surrey were arrested for treason.
- The Earl of Surrey, a poet who helped bring the sonnet to England, was beheaded on January 19, 1547. His father, the Duke of Norfolk, was saved only because the king died before the execution could be carried out.
- Henry's will left the throne to Edward, then Mary, then Elizabeth. If all three died without children, it skipped the Scottish family of his older sister Margaret in favor of the English family of his younger sister Mary.
- Henry VIII died at Whitehall Palace in the early hours of January 28, 1547, at the age of 55. He had reigned for nearly 38 years.

### After Henry

- Henry's death was kept secret for several days while Edward Seymour, the new king's uncle, took control. Seymour won over the royal council by handing out lands and titles that he claimed Henry had meant them to have, and made himself Lord Protector.
- Henry was buried at Windsor Castle beside Jane Seymour, and the grand tomb he had planned for himself was never finished. Within months, Catherine Parr married Thomas Seymour, the man she had wanted all along.
- Henry left three children: Edward (age 9), Mary (age 30), and Elizabeth (age 13). All three went on to rule England.
- Henry had six wives. English schoolchildren remember their fates with the rhyme "divorced, beheaded, died, divorced, beheaded, survived."
- Henry left England with its own national church, a stronger navy, and a more powerful Parliament. He never settled what his church should believe, however, and he executed both Catholics and Protestants. The British monarch is still the head of the Church of England today, with the title "Supreme Governor."
